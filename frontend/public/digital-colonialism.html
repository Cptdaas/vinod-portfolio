Digital Colonialism & What India Must Confront Now
Vinod Tiwari  ·  AI Architect, Deloitte USI  ·  Writing as an Indian engineer inside the machine

In 1700, India accounted for roughly 27% of global GDP. By 1950, after two centuries of British rule, that number had collapsed to 3%. Economist Utsa Patnaik's research estimates the total extraction at $45 trillion — the largest wealth transfer in recorded human history. We called it the British Empire. Historians now call it what it was: systematic colonial extraction dressed in the language of civilization and free trade.

I am an AI engineer. I build LLM pipelines, agentic systems, and RAG architectures for enterprise clients. I work inside the very technology this article is about. And I need to say something that few people with my job title say out loud:

"The architecture of extraction hasn't changed. Only the weapons have."

The Parallel

Same Structure. Different Interface.

Look carefully at what the British Empire actually did — not the military occupation, but the economic architecture. It extracted raw materials cheaply, processed them in British factories, sold finished goods back to Indians at profit, and used Indian tax revenue to fund the army that kept the system running. They called it the civilizing mission. They called it free trade.

Now look at what American tech companies do in India in 2026.

The structure is identical. The weapons are different. Instead of ships and cannons, it is APIs, cloud dependency, and patent law. Instead of trade agreements signed at gunpoint, it is terms of service clicked through on a phone screen.

The Economic Trap

We Are Building Our Own Replacement

India's IT sector — 5.4 million direct employees, $250 billion in exports, 7.5% of GDP — was the single most effective middle-class creation engine in post-liberalization India. A kid from Lucknow or Coimbatore with a B.Tech could get a TCS job, send money home, build a life. That pipeline lifted tens of millions of families.

That pipeline ran on volume. And AI ends volume hiring. Permanently.

The companies deploying this AI capture the productivity gains. The workers displaced by it bear the cost. The profits flow to shareholders in California. The unemployment flows to families in Pune, Hyderabad, and Chennai.

India's data — generated by 1.4 billion people browsing, searching, creating, working — is being used to train models that will be sold back to India at premium dollar prices. We are the raw material. We are also the customer. We are not the owner. The East India Company at least had to send ships. This extraction requires only a subscription.

The Henry Ford Question

Who Will Buy What AI Produces?

In 1914, Henry Ford doubled his workers' wages to $5 a day. People thought he was mad. His answer was simple: "I want my workers to be able to buy my cars." He understood that production without consumption is a dead end. You cannot sell products to people you just made unemployed.

If AI displaces the Indian middle class — the only class that pays full taxes, receives no subsidies, and drives consumer spending — then who buys the AI-produced cars, groceries, software, and services? The rich will sell to each other. The poor cannot afford to buy. The middle, hollowed out, can do neither.

This is not a social justice argument. This is a math problem. And nobody at the top of the system has solved it, because they are optimizing for next quarter's earnings, not next decade's survival.

What Must Change

Three Demands India Must Make — Now, Not Later

1. Data sovereignty is non-negotiable. Indian data generated by Indian citizens must have Indian IP protections. The economic value of that data should not automatically flow to servers in Virginia. This requires policy, not goodwill.

2. India needs its own foundational AI infrastructure. Depending on OpenAI and Google for AI is depending on the East India Company for cotton processing. The investment in BharatGPT, ISRO's AI programs, and IIT research must scale by an order of magnitude — not as prestige projects but as strategic infrastructure.

3. AI productivity gains must be taxed and redistributed domestically. Every company deploying AI in India that displaces Indian workers must contribute to a retraining and transition fund. This is not anti-technology. It is the same logic as taxing industrial pollution — you cannot externalize the full cost of your productivity onto society.

I write this as someone who builds these systems every day. I am not against AI. I am against the unexamined assumption that technological progress automatically distributes its benefits fairly — when history shows, repeatedly and without exception, that it does not.

The British Empire didn't announce itself as extraction. It came with railways, telegraph lines, and the English language — genuine innovations that also happened to serve the purpose of control and profit. The question India failed to ask in time was: in whose interest is this infrastructure being built?

We can ask that question now. While there is still time to shape the answer.
